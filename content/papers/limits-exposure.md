Can structured cross socioeconomic status (SES) collaboration in elite universities
build economic connectedness? We study this question in a field experiment embedded in
a required undergraduate course at Universidad de los Andes during a period of rapidly
rising low-SES enrollment. We map within-section friendship networks and elicit
students' willingness to trade off working with a friend for working with a
higher-performing non-friend, using randomized monetary incentives and randomized SES
information about the potential teammate. We then randomly assign students to essay
teams that vary in SES composition and instrumentally pair them with a random friend to
work with. Baseline networks display strong SES homophily. Making SES salient increases
high-SES students' preference to work only with friends, while low-SES students'
choices become more homophilous at the top of the performance distribution. In realized
teams, SES diversity reduces meeting and satisfaction and lowers willingness to work
together again, with little effect on essay grades. These frictions persist in a
follow-up survey, while friend-pairing mainly benefits low-SES students.
